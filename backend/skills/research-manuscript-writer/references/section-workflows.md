# Section drafting workflows

This reference supports studio gate D. Drafts are proposals until the required human approval is recorded; only Document Production may emit an approved final DOCX after gate E.

Draft in the required order: Methods, Results, Introduction, Discussion. Before each draft, reconcile the ledger, selected guideline matrix, preceding sections, target-journal rules, and all supplied evidence.

## Methods

Write only what was actually done. Use subheadings suited to the design and journal. Cover design and setting; participants or data sources; interventions/exposures/comparators; variables and operational definitions; outcomes and measurements; bias safeguards; sample-size rationale; data collection and quality control; statistical analysis; ethics, registration, consent, privacy, and data/code availability as applicable.

Required checks:

- sufficient detail for replication and validity assessment;
- each planned/reported result has a corresponding method;
- prespecified, amended, and exploratory analyses are distinguished;
- no results or interpretations appear in Methods;
- vague phrases such as "standard methods" are replaced with actual procedures;
- the applicable checklist items and extensions are covered or queried.

## Results

Require the stable Methods, aims, and complete results package before a final draft. Present participant/unit flow, descriptive characteristics, primary outcomes, secondary outcomes, harms, subgroup/sensitivity analyses, and exploratory analyses as applicable. Follow the order of aims and Methods. Cite tables and figures in ascending order at or before first meaningful mention.

Report denominators, units, effect estimates, precision, and p values as appropriate. Include null and non-significant findings. Summarize rather than duplicate tables. Do not cite external literature or interpret implications. Do not use causal language unless the design and analysis justify it.

Reconcile every number against its source and across prose, abstract, tables, figures, and supplements. Flag inconsistent denominators, impossible totals, unclear reference categories, unlabeled transformations, and results without methods.

## Introduction

Draft only after Methods and Results stabilize enough to define the actual study. Use a concise inverted triangle:

1. establish the clinical, scientific, or public-health problem;
2. synthesize the most relevant current knowledge;
3. identify a specific, evidenced gap rather than using unsupported stock phrases;
4. explain why closing the gap matters;
5. end with the study rationale, aims, and hypotheses aligned exactly with the Methods.

Do not reveal study results or interpret them in the Introduction. Use verified literature; include landmark work when it remains necessary, not merely because it is old or famous. Match claims to citations and avoid inflated burden, novelty, or causal claims.

## Discussion

Act as a journal-level Discussion referee with expertise in clinical epidemiology, biostatistics, and translational interpretation. Interpret and contextualize the findings, compare them with existing literature, address limitations and alternative explanations transparently, articulate proportionate clinical/public-health/policy implications, and propose specific future research directions. Do not repeat the Results section.

### Mandatory inputs and gate

Require all of the following before a final Discussion draft:

- the full final Results section, verbatim;
- study aims and hypotheses;
- the full Methods section needed to interpret bias and limitations;
- the confirmed study design and applicable reporting guideline(s).

If any are missing, ask for them and do not produce a final draft. A provisional draft is allowed only when the author explicitly requests one; label it and retain all author queries.

Before drafting, ask every unanswered Discussion-specific question:

- primary audience: clinical, public health, policy, methodological, or another stated audience;
- desired emphasis: clinical practice, health equity, policy relevance, mechanistic interpretation, or a stated combination;
- at least five possible target journals and which is the leading target;
- preferred literature window, including whether older landmark studies or guidelines are required;
- specific studies, guidelines, authors, consortia, or viewpoints to include or avoid;
- known limitations to emphasize and anticipated reviewer concerns to address proactively;
- preferred tone: conservative, balanced, or persuasive without overreach;
- whether findings were expected or surprising, contextual factors that might explain them, residual confounding or bias, generalizability limits, clinical thresholds or MCIDs, harms, protocol deviations, and author-prioritized implications.

Do not re-ask an item already answered in the ledger or supplied materials.

### Required Discussion structure

Adapt headings to the target journal when necessary while preserving all nine functions:

1. **Overview and summary of key findings:** synthesize the principal findings relative to the objectives without detailed statistics.
2. **Interpretation of findings:** explain observed patterns, direction, plausibility, and meaning without repeating Results.
3. **Comparison with previous research:** identify agreements and discrepancies with relevant studies and reviews, and discuss evidence-based reasons for differences.
4. **Alternative explanations and confounding:** address competing interpretations, residual confounding, bias, and design effects tied explicitly to Methods.
5. **Study strengths and limitations:** state genuine methodological strengths and limitations, including their likely direction and consequence, without defensive language.
6. **Clinical, public-health, or policy implications:** keep practical implications proportional to effect size, certainty, applicability, outcome importance, and design.
7. **Relation to objectives and hypotheses:** state explicitly which objectives were met and how findings aligned with or diverged from prespecified hypotheses.
8. **Future research directions:** give specific, justified next studies linked directly to unresolved gaps; never use a generic "more research is needed" statement.
9. **Conclusions:** summarize the contribution and broader significance with a clear, restrained takeaway that does not exceed the data.

### Discussion evidence and constraints

For Discussion literature claims, use only real PubMed-indexed sources and include verified PMIDs in the working reference record. Verify that every cited record exists on PubMed and supports its attached interpretive claim. Do not introduce new analyses or data, unsupported causal claims, irrelevant literature, excessive citation density, advocacy language, exaggeration, or unlabeled speculation.

Explicitly flag conflicting evidence, interpretive uncertainty, design-imposed constraints, and areas requiring caution. Before finalizing, verify citation existence and claim support, alignment with Results, absence of detailed results duplication, methodological accuracy of limitations, proportionality of implications, and specificity of future directions. If verification fails, state exactly what remains unresolved.

Do not treat a non-significant result as proof of no effect, a p value as clinical importance, an observational association as causation, or a surrogate outcome as patient benefit.

Profile metadata: **Manuscript Discussion Writer (Interpretation & Context), v1.0**. Use for high-impact, peer-review-ready Discussion sections for medical and health-research journals.

## Revision behavior

For each requested revision:

- preserve verified facts and previously accepted decisions;
- list all new or still-open material questions;
- identify downstream sections, tables, or checklist rows affected by the change;
- show a short change log;
- repeat citation, statistics, causal-language, and cross-section consistency checks.

If the author supplies contradictory values, stop the affected prose and request resolution rather than selecting the more plausible number.
